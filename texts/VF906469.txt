Miss Me?

by Maureen Ash

In 1938 a fishing boat made a surprising catch—a fish from the time of the dinosaurs!

1 It was the summer of 1938 in South Africa and very, very hot. Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer was sweating as she worked over a fossil she wanted to assemble and display in the small museum where she was director. The phone rang. It was Captain Goosen, a fisherman who tried to help her find new and unusual specimens for the museum. He’d caught a lot of fish—did she want to look at them in case there was something interesting?

2 The pile of fish looked like every other pile of fish she’d seen lately. Except for one just sticking out from the others—it was a lovely blue. Courtenay-Latimer slid the other fish away from it. What she saw was so unusual that she couldn’t think what it could be.

3 It was 5 feet (1.5 meters) long and weighed more than 100 pounds (45 kilograms). A shiny, steely blue, covered in heavy scales, it had the oddest tail, like a fringe that hid another little tail. The fins looked a bit like legs, and scales covered them right up to where they ended in another fringe.

4 Courtenay-Latimer contacted an expert, sending him a letter and her drawing of the fish. Dr. J. L. B. Smith said later of the drawing, “a bomb seemed to burst inside my brain” as he began to realize how closely the fish described in the letter resembled fossils he had seen of fish that had been extinct for 65 million years.

5 The fish was a coelacanth (say it “see-la-canth”), a creature no one had seen before except as a fossil. Since that first one was discovered in 1938, 175 have been caught and studied. Scientists in small submarines have even observed them in their natural habitat, hundreds of feet underwater. Instead of a backbone, a coelacanth has a hollow notochord filled with oil. It can survive for a long time without much food, which means it can live deeper than most fish. Because deep, cold water contains more oxygen, the coelacanth gets by with smaller gills than most fish. Its tiny brain is located behind a special joint that allows it to open its jaws very wide for a powerful bite when it finds prey.

6 Scientists once offered rewards to anyone who caught these rare and special fish. Now scientists are using what they’ve learned to try to keep the coelacanths safe in their deep, cold homes where they have lived for so many millions of years.

Lonesome Pines

7 Coelacanths are not the only living fossils. One day in 1994, David Noble and his friends were exploring in Wollemi National Park, New South Wales, Australia. They used ropes to climb down some cliffs and found a small rainforest tucked away between the steep sandstone walls. Noble knew a lot about plants, but he didn’t recognize the trees he saw there. The bark was dark brown and seemed to have bubbled. The branches didn’t divide into other branches—they just stuck straight out and ended. The flat leaves looked a bit like ferns.

8 He broke off a few leaves and branches and brought them back to find someone who could help him identify the trees. The scientists he showed them to were astounded—they knew the trees only as 2-million-year-old fossils! No one had ever seen one alive. Today fewer than 100 of these trees, called Wollemia nobilis, or Wollemi pines, live in their hidden valley. A program has been started to allow people to grow Wollemi pines for themselves. Growing more of these trees in different places will help to make sure they survive.

Clues to Ancient Life

by Rona Arato

1 Fossils provide a record of life on Earth. Fossils reveal evidence of ancient life that is preserved in sediment or sedimentary rock. Fossils range from tiny plants and animals to the bones of enormous dinosaurs.

Why study fossils?

2 Scientists learn about past life on Earth and how Earth has changed over millions of years from fossils. Fossils tell what animals and plants lived and died out at different times. By examining fossils and the rocks they are found in, scientists understand the effects that events such as mass extinctions, meteorite impacts, and climate change have on Earth’s history. Fossils provide a valuable look into our past, but they do not tell the whole story. Many plants and animals did not become fossils.

Body fossils

3 Body fossils are the whole body or parts of the body of a plant or animal. To become a body fossil, some part of the organism must not decay or rot. Skin and internal organs rot, but bones do not. Plant material rots, so plants occur only as imprint fossils. Most body fossils are found buried in sediment, or layers of rock and soil. In rare cases, extreme cold freezes an organism, similar to the way a freezer preserves food. In very dry conditions, such as deserts, a dead animal loses its moisture and shrivels up.

Trace fossils

4 Trace fossils are markings left behind by an organism such as footprints, trails, burrows, and nests. Scientists learn about the movement and behavior of animals from trace fossils. Coprolites are fossilized animal waste. Paleontologists learn what an animal ate from its coprolite.

Sea fossils

5 Over 2,000 years ago, Greek scientists found fossils of sea life in the Pindus Mountain range, in Greece. They said the fossils proved that the mountains had at one time been under the sea. Most people refused to believe them. Today, scientists know that oceans at one time covered most of Earth then receded, leaving behind dry land. Life began in the sea and has existed about eight times longer than life on land. Many more sea animals than land animals have been preserved.

How fossils form

6 Earth’s crust is made up of different types of rock. Fossils are found in rock. There are three forms of rock: igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic. Rocks are made up of different kinds of minerals. Minerals are solid, non-living substances made of elements.

Sedimentary rock

7 Most fossils are found in sedimentary rock. The word sediment means “something that settles.” Sedimentary rocks are a mixture of dust, sand, mud, shells, corals, and other materials that settle underwater or on land, and compress under pressure. Sedimentary rock forms in layers called strata, with the oldest layer under the newer layers. When a plant or animal dies, it is covered by layers of sediment and preserved as a fossil. Many sedimentary rocks are fossil-rich, while others contain no fossils.

Making fossils

8 Few of the billions of organisms that have lived on Earth became fossils. For fossilization to occur, an organism must contain hard parts, such as a skeleton or a shell. It has to be buried deeply right after it dies, before it decays from exposure to air, water, or bacteria.

Igneous rock

9 Some igneous rocks form when magma rises to the surface through cracks or volcanoes, and cools. Other igneous rocks form when magma crystallizes within Earth’s crust. The word igneous means “fiery.” Igneous rock does not contain fossils because the lava is so hot it burns any animals and plants it touches.

Metamorphic rock

10 Metamorphic rocks are rocks that are changed by heat and pressure. The word metamorphic means “change.” Most metamorphic rocks are fossil-free because the pressures that changed them destroyed all evidence of fossils. Some rocks, such as slate, may contain traces of fossils, although their shapes are very different than when they were alive.
